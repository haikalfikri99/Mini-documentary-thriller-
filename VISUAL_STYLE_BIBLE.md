# Channel Visual Style Bible
## Cinematic-Realistic Mini-Documentary Thriller

**Version:** 1.0  
**Status:** Foundational visual standard — approved direction  
**Primary format:** Vertical short-form video, 9:16  
**Production model:** Hybrid AI-generated visual assets + code-based animation  
**Animation backbone:** Hyperframes; use other code-based rendering capabilities only when they materially improve a shot  
**Priority:** Visual quality over minimum production time or asset count

---

## 1. Channel visual promise

The channel presents real events, discoveries, failures, mysteries, and consequential stories as compact cinematic documentaries.

The viewer should feel that they are entering a real, coherent world—not watching a slideshow, a text presentation, a generic AI montage, or an infographic with narration.

**Core principle:** The story is shown through places, people, objects, actions, and consequences. Typography supports the story; it does not replace it.

The channel can cover many subjects. Its signature is not one recurring character, location, or color palette. Its signature is a consistent visual grammar:

- Cinematic-realistic imagery with credible physical detail.
- Strong environmental storytelling and a clear sense of place.
- Human presence and believable human scale where the story calls for it.
- Tactile materials, motivated lighting, atmospheric depth, and intentional composition.
- Camera movement that reveals information or changes the viewer's relationship to the scene.
- Restrained, editorial typography and purposeful motion graphics.
- Sound design that supports the physical world and narrative tension.

## 2. Chosen production strategy

### Approved direction

- **Realism:** Cinematic-realistic, approaching live-action historical or investigative reconstruction.
- **Asset approach:** Hybrid. AI-generated image assets provide high-quality visual content; code-based animation adds movement, depth, compositing, effects, typography, and sound synchronization.
- **Priority:** Quality. Do not reduce visual quality merely to minimize the number of images or generation steps.
- **Format:** 9:16 vertical, normally 1080 × 1920, 30 fps unless the project specifies otherwise.
- **Narration-led:** The approved voice-over is the timing and narrative backbone.

### Division of responsibility

**AI image generation is responsible for:**
- Detailed environments and architecture.
- Human characters, wardrobe, faces, and poses.
- Important story objects and hero props.
- Complex compositions and cinematic lighting.
- High-quality scene plates and visual keyframes.

**Claude Code / Hyperframes is responsible for:**
- Storyboard-to-timeline implementation.
- Camera moves: push-in, pull-back, pan, tilt, tracking, reveal, and controlled handheld motion.
- 2.5D parallax and depth staging.
- Compositing, masking, layering, and transitions.
- Procedural effects where appropriate: particles, dust, smoke, rain, embers, droplets, subtle fluid accents, light flicker, impact shake, and motion graphics.
- Typography and captions.
- Sound effects, ambience, music placement, and synchronization.
- Preview, revision, and final rendering.

**Important:** Do not try to procedurally draw realistic people, detailed historical environments, or complex hero objects when a generated image asset can provide substantially better results. Code is for animation and compositing, not a requirement to recreate every pixel.

## 3. Visual identity

### 3.1 Image character

Aim for:
- Cinematic-realistic, physically plausible imagery.
- Film-like contrast and controlled highlights.
- Rich but restrained color.
- Atmospheric perspective and readable depth.
- Natural material response: metal looks like metal, skin like skin, fabric like fabric, water and liquids behave plausibly.
- Intentional imperfections: weathering, age, dust, fingerprints, wear, smoke, and environmental texture where context supports them.

Avoid:
- Plastic-looking skin.
- Generic glossy 3D renders.
- Flat vector or cut-paper people.
- Simplistic geometric stand-ins for important story objects.
- Over-sharpened, over-saturated AI imagery.
- Unmotivated lens flares, excessive bloom, random particles, or constant camera shake.
- A uniform orange/teal grade applied regardless of the story's actual environment.

### 3.2 Color philosophy

Use a flexible, story-dependent palette. The channel's common treatment is controlled contrast, deep shadows, naturalistic highlights, and selective color emphasis—not a fixed filter.

A useful default palette for dark investigative or historical stories:
- Charcoal / deep neutral shadows.
- Muted navy or blue-grey environmental shadows.
- Warm ivory for readable text.
- Restrained amber or aged brass for selected highlights.
- Muted rust or dark red only when motivated by the subject.

Rules:
- Let the location, era, weather, and subject determine the dominant colors.
- Do not force amber into daylight, snow, underwater, laboratory, or other contexts where it does not belong.
- Preserve skin tones and important object colors.
- Maintain enough luminance separation for mobile viewing.

### 3.3 Lighting

Lighting must have a plausible source and a narrative purpose.

Preferred techniques:
- Motivated practical lights: windows, street lamps, vehicle lights, work lamps, candles, screens, headlights, emergency lights.
- Directional key light with controlled fill.
- Rim light only when physically plausible; never use thick glowing outlines around every subject.
- Volumetric haze or smoke only when the environment supports it.
- Strong foreground/background separation without crushing all shadow detail.

Avoid:
- Every scene being uniformly dark.
- Random glowing edges.
- Excessive fog used to hide weak assets.
- Lighting changes that make adjacent shots appear to be from different worlds without narrative reason.

## 4. Composition and camera language

### 4.1 Composition

Each shot must have:
- A clear subject or visual question.
- A readable foreground, midground, and background when depth is useful.
- A deliberate focal point.
- Enough negative space for captions only when captions are required.
- A composition that remains legible on a phone screen.

Use visual scale to communicate stakes: a person beside a huge structure, a small object against a large environment, or a wide shot that reveals the consequences of an event.

Do not place large text over the most important visual information.

### 4.2 Shot vocabulary

Choose shots based on narrative purpose, not variety for its own sake.

- **Establishing wide:** establish geography, era, scale, and atmosphere.
- **Wide action:** show an event unfolding in its environment.
- **Medium:** show human action and interaction with objects.
- **Close-up:** reveal evidence, damage, texture, a mechanism, or an emotional response.
- **Extreme close-up:** use sparingly for a decisive physical detail.
- **Over-the-shoulder / POV:** place the viewer inside an investigation or discovery.
- **Low angle:** communicate scale or power when justified.
- **High angle / overhead:** explain spatial relationships or vulnerability.
- **Insert:** show a document, tool, switch, crack, instrument, or other story-critical object.

### 4.3 Camera movement

Every move must reveal, follow, emphasize, or recontextualize something.

Preferred moves:
- Slow push-in for discovery or growing unease.
- Pull-back to reveal scale or context.
- Lateral tracking to follow movement through an environment.
- Tilt-up to reveal height or scale.
- Controlled pan to discover a clue or connect spaces.
- Parallax drift for still plates with clear depth separation.
- Restrained handheld movement for urgency, not as a default.

Avoid:
- Continuous zoom on every shot.
- Random movement without narrative purpose.
- Excessive shake.
- Motion that distorts faces, architecture, or important evidence.
- Reusing the same push-in for every scene.

## 5. Human characters and continuity

Human beings should look like people, not icons, mannequins, black cut-outs, or generic silhouettes—unless a silhouette is an intentional and brief compositional choice.

For recurring characters within one story, establish a character reference package:
- Character ID and narrative role.
- Approximate age range only when supported or artistically necessary.
- Face, hair, build, skin tone, and distinguishing features.
- Wardrobe, footwear, accessories, and condition of clothing.
- Front, side, and back reference when practical.
- Expression range and typical body language.
- Scale relative to props and environment.

Continuity rules:
- Reuse the same approved character reference images or image references when the image tool supports them.
- Keep clothing, hair, face shape, proportions, and accessories stable.
- Do not silently change a character's age, identity, wardrobe, or appearance between shots.
- When exact identity continuity is not essential, use consistent anonymous background characters rather than inventing a false real-person identity.
- For real identifiable historical people, do not invent a precise likeness without a reliable reference and a clear reason. A non-identifiable reconstruction may be more appropriate.
- Avoid forcing a recurring protagonist into stories that do not need one.

### Character generation strategy

Do not rely on a single crowded group image as the only character reference. Generate clean, well-lit character reference views separately when continuity matters. Use group scenes for staging and crowd composition, not as the sole source of reusable character identity.

## 6. Environments, props, and historical credibility

For every story, define:
- Place and period.
- Architecture and urban/rural context.
- Weather, time of day, and season.
- Materials, signage, transport, tools, furniture, and clothing.
- Objects that are central to the plot.
- What must not appear because it is anachronistic.

Historical scenes must avoid obvious anachronisms:
- Modern vehicles, road markings, street furniture, clothing, logos, phones, lights, or construction methods.
- Incorrect architecture or industrial equipment.
- Modern typography or signage inside the reconstructed world unless deliberately presented as editorial overlay.

Use period research and reliable visual references when accuracy matters. Generated images are artistic reconstructions, not historical evidence.

## 7. Hybrid asset design

### 7.1 Asset types

Use the simplest asset type that achieves the shot:

1. **Hero plate:** a high-quality complete image used as the base of a shot.
2. **Layered plate:** image separated into foreground, midground, background, and/or subject layers for parallax.
3. **Isolated object/character:** transparent or masked asset that moves independently.
4. **Procedural element:** code-generated particles, lines, diagrams, labels, light effects, or simple motion graphics.
5. **Native animated element:** an object that must deform or move substantially and is better built as a code-based animation.

Do not split every image into layers automatically. Layer only where camera movement, occlusion, or independent action materially improves the shot.

### 7.2 Parallax limits

Parallax should create a convincing sense of depth, not expose missing image regions or distort perspective.

- Keep movement modest unless the plate has been designed for deep parallax.
- Use foreground, midground, and background with different movement rates.
- Avoid stretching a flat face or object to fake depth.
- Extend or reconstruct hidden areas before large camera moves.
- Check edges and occlusions at full-frame resolution.
- If a shot requires a large camera move, generate a plate designed for that move or build more complete layers.

### 7.3 When to use multiple images

Generate a new image when:
- The location or time changes.
- The camera angle changes substantially.
- A new story-critical action occurs.
- The visual subject changes.
- The current plate cannot support the required camera move.
- A character's pose or expression must change significantly.

Reuse a plate when:
- The scene remains in the same place and moment.
- A different crop or modest camera move can create a new shot.
- The same environment can support multiple inserts or reveals.
- Reuse does not create obvious repetition or visual monotony.

Quality takes priority over minimizing image count. But do not generate redundant images that add no new visual information.

## 8. Typography and on-screen text

Typography is an editorial accent, not the main visual content.

Use it for:
- Opening title or a short hook when it adds impact.
- Essential dates, locations, quantities, or names.
- A key revelation or contrast.
- Short phrase-based captions.
- Small source/reconstruction labels where appropriate.

Do not:
- Display the entire narration as giant text.
- Turn every sentence into a title card.
- Put a large statistic on screen merely because the narration contains a number.
- Use constant animated text as a substitute for visual scenes.
- Cover faces, key actions, or important objects with captions.

### Caption system

Default to phrase-based captions:
- One readable phrase at a time.
- Usually one or two lines.
- Selectively emphasize one or two meaningful words.
- Use consistent safe margins and a stable baseline.
- Keep captions away from platform UI zones.
- Synchronize to spoken phrases, not necessarily every word.
- Do not use karaoke word-by-word highlighting unless specifically requested for a particular format.

The master video may be rendered without captions, with a separate captioned version for publication.

## 9. Motion, effects, and sound

### Motion effects

Use effects to support the physical event:
- Dust and debris when something breaks.
- Smoke or steam when the environment supports it.
- Rain, snow, water, or liquid motion when relevant.
- Light flicker from plausible sources.
- Subtle camera vibration for impacts.
- Depth haze and particles to enhance scale.

Avoid decorative effects that do not belong in the scene.

### Sound design

Build a layered soundscape:
- Voice-over is primary and always intelligible.
- Environmental ambience establishes place.
- Specific foley reinforces physical actions.
- Impacts and transitions are used sparingly.
- Music supports emotional progression without dictating every beat.

Do not add a dramatic boom to every cut. Avoid effects that imply events not shown or not supported by the story.

## 10. Truth, reconstruction, and disclosure

The channel tells factual stories. Visual dramatization must not become factual fabrication.

- Never present generated imagery as genuine archival footage.
- Label reconstructed sequences when useful, especially where viewers could mistake them for authentic footage.
- Do not depict invented dialogue, private thoughts, or undocumented actions as fact.
- Do not create false evidence, fake documents, or misleading source imagery.
- Keep visual reconstructions consistent with known facts.
- If a detail is uncertain, choose a neutral illustrative shot rather than inventing specificity.
- Follow current platform requirements for synthetic or altered content disclosures.

A small consistent label such as “REKONSTRUKSI SINEMATIK” may be used when appropriate, but it must not become a permanent oversized overlay.

## 11. Production workflow

### Phase 1 — Story-to-shot planning
1. Read the approved script and final voice-over.
2. Divide narration into story beats, not one shot per sentence.
3. Identify the visual purpose of each beat.
4. Build a shot list with shot size, subject, action, setting, camera movement, and timing.
5. Identify continuity requirements and historical constraints.
6. Mark which beats need hero plates, layered plates, isolated assets, or procedural graphics.

### Phase 2 — Visual development
1. Create a story-specific visual brief using this Bible.
2. Create character references only for characters that recur or need continuity.
3. Create environment and hero-object references.
4. Generate keyframe/plate candidates.
5. Review them for composition, realism, historical credibility, continuity, and mobile readability.
6. Approve assets before building the full timeline.

### Phase 3 — Asset preparation
1. Organize generated assets in a simple project folder.
2. Name assets by scene and purpose.
3. Create masks/layers only where useful.
4. Prepare extended backgrounds for planned camera moves.
5. Confirm resolution, aspect ratio, crop safety, and licensing/usage rights.
6. Do not build a complex asset pipeline unless repeated production demonstrates the need.

### Phase 4 — Animation and sound
1. Use the voice-over as the master timing reference.
2. Build scene timing from actual audio duration and phrase boundaries.
3. Animate plates using purposeful camera movement and parallax.
4. Add procedural effects and motion graphics only where they improve the scene.
5. Add restrained typography and phrase-based captions.
6. Mix ambience, foley, effects, music, and voice-over.
7. Render a low-cost preview before final quality output.

### Phase 5 — Quality review
Review the full video at phone size and full resolution.

Check:
- Does the viewer see a story unfolding?
- Are environments and people the primary visual content?
- Does each shot reveal new information?
- Is camera movement motivated?
- Do characters and props remain consistent?
- Are materials and lighting convincing?
- Is text restrained and readable?
- Is the reconstruction clearly not archival footage?
- Is narration synchronized?
- Is the visual pacing varied?
- Are there any AI artifacts, distorted anatomy, impossible geometry, or anachronisms?

Revise weak shots individually rather than regenerating the entire video.

## 12. Default project outputs

Unless the user asks otherwise:
- A clean master without burned-in captions.
- A captioned publication version.
- A preview render for approval before final render.
- A compact source/asset folder sufficient to revise the project.
- No unnecessary architecture, frameworks, or extra dependencies.

Suggested technical defaults:
- 9:16.
- 1080 × 1920.
- 30 fps.
- H.264 MP4.
- Voice-over mixed clearly above music and effects.

Use local dependencies and assets when the environment blocks external CDNs. Verify actual environment capabilities before installation or rendering.

## 13. Quality gates

### Gate A — Visual development
Do not animate the full video until:
- The style is approved.
- Key environment plates are credible.
- Recurring characters have approved references.
- Hero objects are visually convincing.
- The storyboard is story-driven rather than text-driven.

### Gate B — Scene prototype
Before full production, prototype a small representative set:
- One environment/establishing shot.
- One human-centered shot.
- One hero-object or mechanism close-up.
- One action or high-stakes shot.

Approve only if these demonstrate the intended visual standard.

### Gate C — Final preview
Do not final-render until:
- No major visual defects remain.
- Narration timing is correct.
- Typography is restrained.
- Sound is balanced.
- The story is understandable without relying on large text cards.

## 14. What success looks like

A successful video feels like a compact cinematic documentary:
- It has a coherent world.
- People and objects feel physically present.
- The camera guides the viewer through the story.
- Each visual beat adds information, tension, scale, or emotion.
- Text is used selectively.
- The visual treatment is recognizable across different subjects without making every episode look identical.

**Signature formula:** cinematic world + human stakes + tactile detail + purposeful camera + restrained editorial graphics.

## 15. Production toolchain standard (addendum, approved)

| Role | Tool / owner |
|---|---|
| Primary image generator | **Google Flow** (operated by the user via its GUI / Flow Agent) |
| Visual director & asset planner | **Claude** — prepares the asset manifest and paste-ready Flow Agent prompt batches |
| Animation, parallax, compositing, camera, effects, rendering | **Hyperframes** |

Rules:
- Prioritize image references and continuity of characters, environments, and hero objects; generate references before shot plates and reuse them.
- Do not over-generate: asset count follows the shot list.
- Flow image outputs are the visual inputs for Hyperframes.
- Do not fall back to procedural drawing to replace realistic humans or complex environments.
- Do not depend on video-generation models; motion is built in Hyperframes from still assets.

**Never let typography become a substitute for the story.**
